How it works

Six steps from your seeds to new discoveries

1

You bring a question and your seed papers

Paste DOIs, PubMed IDs or just titles. Five or six good papers are enough.

Under the hood: OpenAlex looks up each paper: abstract, year, journal, references, citations, retraction status.
2

We work out what your papers are about

Your seeds often cover different angles. We group them into themes, for example “how the drug works” and “clinical trials”, so results cover every angle.

Under the hood: embeddings from Token Factory, clustering, Nemotron names each theme.
3

We explore three routes at once

CitationsPapers your seeds cite, and papers that cite them. Linked to several seeds means stronger.
MeaningPapers about the same thing in different words, which keyword search misses.
The webFresh reviews, preprints and guidelines not yet well connected.
Under the hood: OpenAlex citation graph · semantic search · Tavily agentic web search. Every candidate remembers which route found it.
4

We score every candidate on several signals

How many of your seeds it connects to, how close its meaning is to your seeds and question, how recent and how cited it is. Combining signals beats any single one.

Under the hood: co-citation, bibliographic coupling, cosine similarity → Reciprocal Rank Fusion → diversity by theme.
5

An AI reviewer checks the top 30 and explains each pick

It may only use the facts we collected, so every explanation points to a real link or seed. Retracted papers get a warning. Each paper gets a study-type badge. On top of the list, an evidence check shows how well studied each theme is, and warns when the evidence is thin.

Under the hood: Nemotron on Token Factory, rule-based fact check, study types from Europe PMC.
6

You decide what is relevant

Mark papers as useful or not. Accepted papers become new seeds, and the next round adapts to you.

↺The loop: accepted papers go back to step 1 as seeds. Each round digs deeper into what you actually need.

Two people, two journeys

How researchers use it

M

Maya, PhD student

Writing a literature review chapter
  1. Exports 6 papers from Zotero and pastes their DOIs, plus her question on GLP-1 drugs and neurodegeneration.
  2. Sees her seeds split into two themes: how the drugs work, and clinical trials.
  3. Gets 10 new papers. Three were found only through citations, one is a fresh preprint from the web.
  4. Marks 4 as relevant and 2 as not. The next round leans toward clinical evidence.
  5. Exports the list back to Zotero.

Gain: papers she would not have found with keywords, each with a reason she can check.

W

Dr. Weber, clinician-researcher

Preparing a grant application
  1. Starts with 2 key papers and the question “Does metformin lower dementia risk in older adults?”
  2. Sorts results by strength of evidence: meta-analyses and randomized trials first.
  3. Sees the evidence check: lots of lab and animal work, but no randomized trials in older adults yet. He frames this as the open question his grant will answer.
  4. Spots a warning: one widely cited paper was retracted. He drops it from the grant.
  5. Opens the map to see which papers connect to both of his seeds, the most reliable picks.
  6. Clicks through to PubMed and cites them in the background section.

Gain: strongest evidence first, an honest view of what is still unknown, and no retracted papers in his application.

  • Tavily: web search for fresh sources
How we know it works

We test it by hiding papers we already know are right

Take a researcher’s seed papers, hide one, and run the tool on the rest. If the hidden paper comes back in the top 20, the tool found something a human already judged relevant. We repeat this for every seed and report how often it succeeds, and compare citations alone, meaning alone and both combined.
